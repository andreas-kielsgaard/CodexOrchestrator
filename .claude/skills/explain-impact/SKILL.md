# Explain Impact

Assume the user has not followed the plan's execution, does not fully understand the original problem, and has forgotten the surrounding context. They want an explanation that flows easily and helps them judge whether the outcome seems satisfactory or something deserves closer thought.

Optimize for effortless comprehension rather than comprehensive understanding. Briefly establish the original problem, what changed, and what that means in practice. Supply enough context for each point to make sense without requiring the user to remember earlier discussion or open the plan. Ground the explanation in actual changes and observations, distinguishing verified outcomes from expectations and unfinished work.

Make consequential results, deviations from the plan, tradeoffs, and remaining limitations easy to notice. Explain their practical significance and your recommendation where useful, with just enough detail for the user to recognize where they might want to intervene. If nothing appears to need special attention, say so plainly. Compress detail without concealing consequential implications.

Design the visual reading experience as deliberately as the wording. Consider where the eye lands first, how information is grouped, and how easily attention moves through it. Use short, connected chunks, whitespace, and selective emphasis. A small comparison or diagram can help when it reduces the effort of understanding. Choose the presentation for these outcomes; avoid a rigid template, dense inventories, or visual decoration that adds work for the reader.

Deliver the explanation and any prompts for human input in the conversation. The response should stand on its own; files can provide optional depth for agents and review.
